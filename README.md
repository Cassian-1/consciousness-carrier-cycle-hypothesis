The Hypothesis of Consciousness-Carrier Cycling: Philosophical Reflections on the Continuity of Consciousness, Individual Autonomy, and the Meaning of Life

summary
The nature of human consciousness and its ultimate fate after death have long been subjects of inquiry in both philosophy and science. Through consciousness, humans perceive the world, form memories, experience emotions, and develop an awareness of their own existence. However, there is currently no consensus on whether subjective experience ceases upon the end of life, or whether there exists a vehicle for consciousness capable of detaching from the original life and continuing into another.
This paper proposes a philosophical thought experiment known as the "Consciousness-Carrier Cycle Hypothesis." The hypothesis posits the existence of an entity—provisionally designated as "X"—that possesses subjective experience and self-awareness. X experiences vision, hearing, and emotions through a human life, though its subjective experiences need not be identical to those of the host. Incapable of performing physical actions in the conventional sense on its own, X derives its existential significance from establishing a connection with a human life. Upon the host's death, X departs from the original host and connects with a new life. While X does not retain personal memories from the previous life, past experiences may leave traces that persist beyond conscious recall.
This hypothesis does not posit that the different hosts are the same individual, nor does it require the new life to inherit the memories and personality of the previous one. Its core argument is that the independence of personal identity and the continuous existence of a consciousness-bearing medium can be conceptually distinguished. This paper further explores the implications of the hypothesis regarding the mind-body relationship, personal identity, memory continuity, the meaning of life, and the concept of immortality, while noting that it remains a philosophical conjecture lacking reliable scientific evidence to substantiate its actual existence.

I. Introduction
Death is an inescapable reality for every living being. When contemplating death, people are concerned not only with the cessation of physical life but also with a deeper question: what becomes of that conscious self—the entity that once perceived the world, experienced emotions, and recognized its own existence—when life comes to an end?

A human life is composed of a multitude of unique experiences. We are capable of remembering the past, comprehending the present, and envisioning the future, forging a distinct personality and identity through our lived experiences. Consequently, death signifies more than just the conclusion of a biological process; it raises the question of whether subjective experience itself comes to an end.
A common understanding holds that consciousness is intimately linked to brain activity and ceases irreversibly when the physiological processes generating it come to an end. However, questions regarding why consciousness entails subjective experience, how personal identity is formed, and the nature of the relationship between consciousness and matter remain subjects worthy of philosophical inquiry.

This article does not attempt to directly prove that consciousness persists after death; instead, it proposes a hypothesis to explore an alternative conceptual framework: if there exists a vehicle of consciousness—independent of any specific host—capable of acquiring experience and meaning through a human life, are the end of that individual life and the end of the vehicle necessarily one and the same?
Based on this, this paper proposes the "Consciousness Carrier Cycle Hypothesis," attempting to conceptualize a model in which a consciousness carrier persists across different lives without negating individual uniqueness.

II. Basic Definition of the Consciousness Carrier Cycle Hypothesis

2.1 Definition of Consciousness Carrier X

This paper refers to the hypothesized entity as X.

X is a subject that possesses subjective experience and is aware of its own existence, yet is unable to independently perform physical activities in the conventional sense. Through its connection to a human life, it can experience the host's visual, auditory, and emotional information; however, these experiences within X's consciousness need not be identical to those of the host.
In this thought experiment, X and the host are two mutually independent conscious entities. The host possesses their own memories, personality, emotions, and self-awareness, and is unaware of X's existence. X, meanwhile, is aware of both the host and their own existence but cannot directly prove their presence to the host, nor can they independently control the host's body.

Thus, X is not merely a component of the host's personality, memories, or self-identity; rather, within the hypothesis, X is an entity that co-exists alongside human consciousness.

It must be emphasized that this definition is a theoretical construct rather than a description of an entity actually discovered in reality.

2.2 The Relationship Between X and Human Life

The existence of X does not depend on any specific individual, yet its significance relies on establishing a connection with human life.

If X were to be severed from all hosts, it could still possess subjective experience and remain aware of its own existence; however, it would be unable to engage in activities in the conventional sense through human life, nor could it realize the relationships and experiences it requires.
X’s relationship with humanity is not limited to any specific individual but extends to human life as a whole; each host serves as a concrete channel through which X engages with the human world.

However, this does not imply that X can simultaneously experience the lives of all people, nor does it mean that merely by being connected to humanity as a whole, it gains access to everyone’s memories and experiences. What X is able to experience remains contingent upon the life experiences it actually encounters.

2.3 The Cyclic Mechanism of the Consciousness Carrier

This hypothesis proposes the following cycle:

First, X establishes a connection with an individual and experiences sensory information and emotions through that person's life experiences.

Second, throughout the host's lifetime, the host and X maintain distinct, independent subjective perspectives. The host lives according to their own consciousness, memories, and judgment, while X experiences this life as a separate entity.

Third, upon the host's death, X departs from the original host.

Fourth, X establishes a connection with a new life and experiences another life through the new host.

Fifth, X does not retain the personal memories of the previous host, though the prior experience may leave an impact on a non-memory-based level.
Thus, the existence of X constitutes a cycle that spans different hosts.

The core of this cycle is not the infinite continuation of a single individual's personality, but rather the concept of a conscious subject capable of maintaining a continuous existence across different lives.

III. Consciousness and the Body: Can Two Subjects Coexist?

The hypothesis of a circulating consciousness carrier raises a significant question: Is it possible for a single body to be linked to two mutually independent conscious subjects?

In everyday experience, people generally regard the body, consciousness, and personal identity as an integrated whole. A person’s vision, hearing, emotions, memories, and behaviors are typically understood as the mental activities of a single individual.
However, this hypothesis draws a conceptual distinction between the conscious subject and the source of sensory information.

In this model, the host experiences the world through their brain and sensory systems, while X—by virtue of its connection to the host—gains access to the corresponding sensory and emotional experiences. Although both have access to the same information, they do not necessarily form identical subjective impressions.

For instance, when the host experiences sadness, X may perceive information related to that sadness without necessarily feeling the exact same emotion. The host's sadness might be rooted in personal memories, relationships, and their interpretation of events, whereas X's experience of this information could possess a different subjective quality.

Thus, the hypothesis distinguishes between three concepts:

1. The source of information is the same.
2. Subjective experiences are interconnected.
3. The conscious subjects are identical.

The first two points do not automatically entail the third. Even if two consciousnesses access the same sensory information, it does not imply that they are the same conscious subject.

However, this framework raises an unresolved mechanistic question: if X is a conscious subject independent of the host, how does it acquire the host's sensory and emotional information? Does this connection involve a describable physical mechanism?

At present, the hypothesis offers no specific mechanism to answer this question. Consequently, while it serves as a subject for philosophical thought experiments, it cannot be regarded as a scientifically validated theory of consciousness.

IV. Memory, Personal Identity, and the Continuity of Consciousness

4.1 Memory Is Not Identical to Consciousness Itself

Memory is a crucial component of personal identity. By recalling past experiences, individuals construct an understanding of their own history and link their selves across different points in time.

However, the "consciousness-bearer cycle hypothesis" proposes that an entity—let us call it X—could continue to exist even if it were unable to recall the past.

This implies that the hypothesis distinguishes between the continuity of memory and the continuity of existence.

Continuity of memory refers to a subject's ability to retain and recall past experiences; continuity of existence refers to the subject's continued presence within the theoretical framework, even in the absence of the ability to recall its own past.

The two are not necessarily the same.
For instance, under this hypothesis, after leaving one host, X enters another. It cannot recall its previous life, yet it remains the same X. Its continuity of identity is guaranteed by the continuity of the entity itself—as defined by the hypothesis—rather than by personal memory.

Thus, the absence of memory does not automatically imply the non-existence of the entity, nor does it prove that the entity has transformed into a different one.

4.2 The Influence of Experiences Without Memory

The hypothesis further posits that while X retains no personal memories of its previous host, past experiences may leave traces that cannot be consciously recalled.

This is a premise that requires further definition.

If past experiences do indeed alter X, such changes must manifest in some form—such as internal states, behavioral predispositions, or other undefined forms of continuity. If X underwent absolutely no change in state or carried over no influence whatsoever after switching hosts, it would be difficult to claim that past experiences continued to affect it.

Consequently, the hypothesis allows for a concept of "historical continuity without memory": X remains unaware of its past, yet may, in a certain sense, still be influenced by it.

This concept helps distinguish between recallable personal history and the lingering effects of experiences that cannot be consciously retrieved; however, it does not in itself prove that consciousness in reality is capable of preserving experience in this manner.

4.3 Does the continuity of X imply the continuity of the person?

This question is central to the entire hypothesis.

If X persists across different hosts, then what endures in this hypothesis is X, not necessarily the personal identity of the original host.

A new host need not inherit the memories, personality, or self-identity of the previous host. Consequently, the two hosts are not the same person, nor do they merge into a single conscious entity simply by virtue of their connection to the same X.

This implies that the cycle of X cannot be directly equated with reincarnation in the conventional sense.

The hypothesis proposes a different possibility: a certain vehicle of consciousness persists, while each specific human being remains an independent and unique individual.

V. The Meaning of Life: Why Does X Need Humans?

In the "consciousness-carrier cycle" hypothesis, X requires humans not only to provide sensory information but also to derive meaning through the establishment of relationships with human lives.

This premise distinguishes existence from meaning.

Even if X is capable of independent existence, this does not imply that it has realized its own meaning. It may be aware of its own existence, yet it cannot independently participate in the actions, relationships, and experiences that constitute human life.

Only by forging connections with humans can X gain access to the concrete trajectory of a life—including human emotions, choices, growth, and relationships.

Consequently, the relationship between X and humanity is not merely instrumental. Humans do not simply provide X with a means to experience the world; they are also the relational partners through whom X acquires meaning.

At the same time, the fact that X establishes a relationship with humanity as a whole does not mean that any specific individual is interchangeable with another. Each host possesses a unique, irreplaceable life history.

While X may engage with different facets of human life through different individuals, every human life retains its own specific character. Thus, the hypothesis allows for the simultaneous coexistence of X’s cyclical continuity and the uniqueness of individual lives.

This perspective also raises a broader philosophical question: Can a subject exist independently, yet require relationships with other forms of life to realize its meaning?

The hypothesis offers an affirmative, model-based answer to this question; however, this answer constitutes a conceptual construct rather than a proof regarding the actual mode of existence of conscious entities.

VI. The Hypothesis of Consciousness-Carrier Cycling and the Concept of Immortality

In the traditional sense, immortality often implies that the same individual continues to exist indefinitely, retaining their memories, personality, and self-identity.

However, the hypothesis of consciousness-carrier cycling adopts a different definition.

It does not require every individual to retain all their memories, nor does it demand the indefinite extension of a specific person's personality. The immortality it envisions is the continued existence of "X"—as a form of conscious subject—across different hosts.

Consequently, the hypothesis distinguishes between three types of continuity:

First, physiological continuity: the continued survival of the same physical body.

Second, continuity of personal identity: the persistence of the same individual's memories, personality, and self-identity.

Third, continuity of the consciousness carrier: the continued existence of a conscious subject across different lives.

In this hypothesis, the third type of continuity does not require the first two to hold true simultaneously.

This is the hypothesis's most distinctive feature: it attempts to preserve a form of consciousness continuity that transcends the end of a life, without requiring the indefinite continuation of personal identity.

However, this definition also gives rise to a philosophical debate.

If the continuation of "X" does not entail the persistence of the original individual's memories, personality, or self-identity, can it truly be termed "human immortality"?

From the perspective of "X," this can be understood as the continued existence of a conscious subject; yet from the perspective of a specific individual, it does not necessarily mean that the person themselves continues to live after death.

Therefore, the "immortality" discussed here refers to a specific theoretical definition rather than a proven mode of post-mortem existence.

VII. Key Issues and Limitations of the Theory

While the "consciousness-carrier cycle hypothesis" possesses a certain conceptual structure, it faces several significant challenges before it can evolve into a more complete theory.

7.1 How does X establish a connection with the brain?

The hypothesis posits that X can experience the host's sensory inputs and emotions, yet it fails to explain the mechanism behind this connection. In particular, given that X is defined as an independent conscious entity, the mechanism for information transfer between X and the brain remains unknown.

7.2 What proves that X maintains its identity across different hosts?

The hypothesis presupposes the ontological continuity of X, but this premise itself requires explanation. In the absence of memory continuity or observable markers of identity, how can one determine that X at different stages is the same entity rather than two distinct ones?

7.3 Can the existence of X be empirically tested?

For a theory to qualify as scientific, it must advance claims that are observable, testable, and potentially falsifiable under certain conditions.

At present, the consciousness-carrier cycle hypothesis offers no methods to independently verify the existence of X, detect its transfer, or confirm its retention of non-memory-related influences.

Consequently, it is currently better suited as a philosophical thought experiment than as a scientifically validated hypothesis.

7.4 Does the unexplained nature of consciousness support the consciousness cycle hypothesis?

Certain aspects of consciousness remain unexplained; however, there is a logical distinction between "not yet explained" and "proven to be capable of cycling."

While unresolved questions in consciousness research can serve as a starting point for new inquiries, they cannot, in isolation, prove the existence of a specific type of consciousness carrier.

Therefore, this paper does not cite the fact that science has yet to fully explain consciousness as evidence for the necessary existence of X; rather, it treats this fact as the backdrop for further exploration into the relationship between consciousness and personal identity.
VIII. Conclusion

The "consciousness-carrier cycle" hypothesis proposes a philosophical model regarding the continuity of consciousness: there exists an entity, X, possessing subjective experience and self-awareness. While X cannot independently perform physical activities in the conventional sense, it can experience the world and derive meaning by establishing a connection with a human life. Upon the death of a host, X departs from the former host and connects with a new life. X does not retain personal memories from the previous life, though past experiences may leave traces that persist beyond conscious recall.

The hypothesis’s most significant conceptual feature is its distinction between the continuous existence of the consciousness carrier and the continuity of personal identity. It does not require different hosts to be the same person, nor does it necessitate that a new life inherit the memories and personality of the preceding one. Each individual retains an independent and unique life, while the continuity of X operates at a different theoretical level.

Philosophically, this hypothesis touches upon the relationship between consciousness and the body, the distinction between memory and identity, the historicity of experience, the nature of relationships and meaning, and varying definitions of immortality. It seeks not to answer how a single person’s life might be infinitely extended, but rather whether a specific subject of consciousness can persist across different lives.

However, the existence of X, its connection to the brain, and its transfer following a host's death currently lack reliable scientific evidence. Consequently, this hypothesis cannot be regarded as a proven theory of consciousness, nor can it be used to assert that human consciousness necessarily persists after death.

Its value lies primarily in raising questions, clarifying concepts, and exploring logical possibilities. It reminds us that discussions regarding death and immortality require distinguishing between the continuity of the body, the continuity of personal identity, and the continuity of a subject of consciousness.

This paper does not provide a verified, definitive answer to the question of where consciousness goes after death. Instead, it offers a conceptual model for further discussion, suggesting that if a cycle of consciousness carriers does indeed exist, the resulting immortality might not entail an individual forever retaining their original identity, but rather the persistence of a subject of consciousness across different lives.

This remains a philosophical possibility awaiting substantiation, rather than a scientifically confirmed fact.
